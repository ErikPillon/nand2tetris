## 1.1 The object of study

Fix $n$ and consider functions $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$. There are
$2^{2^n}$ of them, since a function is exactly a choice of output for each of
the $2^n$ input rows. For $n = 1$ there are 4; for $n = 2$, 16; for $n = 3$,
256; for $n = 4$, 65,536.

That finiteness is the whole reason hardware is possible. A Boolean function
*is* its truth table — a finite object, with no limit process, no convergence,
no approximation. Building hardware means realising a chosen table as a
physical device. Nothing is lost in translation.

All sixteen functions of two variables, for reference. The table is written as
the column of outputs for inputs $(0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)$:

| table | name | table | name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0000 | constant 0 | 1000 | Nor |
| 0001 | **And** | 1001 | Xnor (equality) |
| 0010 | $a \wedge \neg b$ | 1010 | $\neg b$ |
| 0011 | $a$ | 1011 | $b \Rightarrow a$ |
| 0100 | $\neg a \wedge b$ | 1100 | $\neg a$ |
| 0101 | $b$ | 1101 | $a \Rightarrow b$ |
| 0110 | **Xor** | 1110 | **Nand** |
| 0111 | **Or** | 1111 | constant 1 |

You will build the four in bold (one as a primitive, three by hand) and, in
`test_80_algebra.py`, watch all sixteen fall out as compositions.

## 1.2 Functional completeness

**Definition.** A set $B$ of Boolean functions is *functionally complete* if
every Boolean function, of every arity, is a composition of members of $B$.

**Proposition.** $\{\wedge, \vee, \neg\}$ is functionally complete.

*Proof.* Let $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$. For $x \in \{0,1\}^n$ define the
minterm
$$m_x(y) \;=\; \bigwedge_{i=1}^{n} \ell_i(y_i), \qquad
\ell_i(y_i) = \begin{cases} y_i & x_i = 1\\ \neg y_i & x_i = 0.\end{cases}$$
Then $m_x(y) = 1$ iff $y = x$: the minterm is the indicator of a single point
of the cube. Hence
$$f \;=\; \bigvee_{x \,:\, f(x) = 1} m_x,$$
a finite disjunction, because the cube is finite. (If $f \equiv 0$ the
disjunction is empty; use the constant 0, which is $y_1 \wedge \neg y_1$.) $\square$

This is *disjunctive normal form*, and it is the mathematical content of the
sentence "a computer can compute anything computable by a circuit". It is also
constructive in the most useless possible way: a DNF for an $n$-input function
can need $2^{n-1}$ minterms. The art of hardware is finding the small
composition, not the canonical one.

**Proposition.** $\{\text{nand}\}$ alone is functionally complete.

*Proof.* Write $a \barwedge b$ for nand. Then

$$\neg a = a \barwedge a, \qquad
a \wedge b = \neg(a \barwedge b) = (a \barwedge b) \barwedge (a \barwedge b), \qquad
a \vee b = \neg a \barwedge \neg b,$$

the last by De Morgan. So $\{\wedge, \vee, \neg\}$ is generated by nand, and
the previous proposition finishes it. $\square$

**That proof is your first three chips.** Lines 1, 2 and 3 of the proof are
`not_`, `and_` and `or_`. The test suite will then evaluate the DNF
construction of the first proposition using *your* gates and check that all
sixteen two-variable functions come out right. You are not taking the theorem
on faith; you are compiling it.

### Two exercises worth doing on paper first

**(a) Nor is also complete.** Find $\neg a$, $a \wedge b$, $a \vee b$ in terms
of nor alone. (Dualise the argument above.)

**(b) $\{\wedge, \vee\}$ is *not* complete.** Call $f$ *monotone* if
$x \le y$ pointwise implies $f(x) \le f(y)$. Show $\wedge$ and $\vee$ are
monotone, and that a composition of monotone functions is monotone. Since
$\neg$ is not monotone, no composition of $\wedge$ and $\vee$ equals it.

Exercise (b) is the real content of the lesson: it tells you *why* negation
cannot be dispensed with, and therefore why CMOS logic being natively
inverting is a gift rather than an inconvenience. `test_80_algebra.py`
contains the monotonicity argument as a runnable test.

## 1.3 The chips

Work in this order. The order is not arbitrary — each chip is built from the
ones above it.

### Step 1–4 — Not, And, Or, Xor

The first three are the three lines of the completeness proof. Transcribe
them.

`xor` is the first chip you have to think about. The obvious route is the DNF:
$a\overline{b} \vee \overline{a}b$, which works and costs 9 NANDs. The budget
allows it. But there is a 4-NAND construction, and finding it teaches the one
optimisation technique that matters in logic design: **compute a shared
subexpression once and fan it out to several places.** Let
$t = a \barwedge b$ and ask what $(a \barwedge t) \barwedge (b \barwedge t)$
expands to. Verify it by hand on all four rows before you type it.

Xor matters out of proportion to its size. It is addition in $\mathbb{F}_2$:
with `xor` as $+$ and `and_` as $\times$, the set $\{0,1\}$ becomes a field,
and `test_04_xor.py` checks the axioms against your gates. Next week it becomes
the sum bit of the adder, and the adder is the ALU, and the ALU is the CPU.

### Step 5–6 — Mux, DMux

`mux(a, b, sel)` returns `a` when `sel` is 0 and `b` when `sel` is 1.

Stop and appreciate this chip. It is `if` as a physical object. A circuit has
no control flow: every gate in it is always computing, always, on whatever is
on its inputs. "Choosing" is not something a circuit *does* — it is something a
circuit *selects from among things it already computed*. Both branches are
always evaluated; the multiplexor merely decides which result is allowed
onward.

That is the deepest difference between hardware and the programming you know,
and it is worth sitting with for a minute. Everything programmable in the
machine you are about to build — every conditional jump, every register write,
every addressing decision, the entire instruction decoder — is multiplexors
being steered by bits.

The canonical form is $(a \wedge \neg s) \vee (b \wedge s)$: 8 NANDs through
your existing gates, 4 if you expand by hand and share the inverted selector.

`dmux` is the converse: one input, two possible destinations, the unselected
one held at 0. Mux gathers, DMux scatters. `test_06_dmux.py` checks that they
are mutually inverse, which is the property that will let Project 3 write to
one register out of eight.

### Step 7–10 — Not16, And16, Or16, Mux16

Sixteen independent copies of one gate, side by side. No new logic, and the
implementations are one line each. Two things to be careful about:

- Return a **tuple**, not a list or a generator.
- `mux16` takes a *single* selector bit that steers all sixteen lanes — not a
  bus of sixteen selectors.

The budget tests here are checking that you reused `not_`, `and_`, `or_`,
`mux` rather than writing out NANDs.

### Step 11 — Or8Way

An 8-wire bus in, one bit out: 1 iff any wire is 1. Note the width — 8, not
16. In Project 5 this chip answers "is this word zero?" for the ALU's status
flags, which is how a conditional jump knows whether to jump.

Build it as a chain (or a tree) of `or_`. Seven of them, 21 NANDs.

### Step 12–13 — Mux4Way16, Mux8Way16

**Read the selector convention twice.** `sel` is a bus, so `sel[0]` is the low
bit. Written as a numeral the bits appear in the opposite order:

| written | `sel` tuple | selects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `"00"` | `(0, 0)` | a |
| `"01"` | `(1, 0)` | b |
| `"10"` | `(0, 1)` | c |
| `"11"` | `(1, 1)` | d |

This is where almost everyone loses an hour. The test failure message prints
both the tuple and the numeral; if `b` and `c` are swapped, you have the
convention backwards.

Build `mux4way16` as a **two-level tree**: `sel[0]` chooses within each pair,
`sel[1]` chooses between the pairs. Three `Mux16` calls, 192 NANDs. Build
`mux8way16` from two `Mux4Way16` and one `Mux16`. Resist every urge to write
these out gate by gate; the budget will stop you anyway.

### Step 14–15 — DMux4Way, DMux8Way

The mirror image, and the chips that make memory addressable. `dmux8way` takes
one bit and a 3-bit address and produces eight outputs of which exactly one can
be 1 — a *one-hot decoder*. In Project 3 that input bit will be a "write
enable" signal and the eight outputs will each be the write-enable of one
register, which is how `RAM8` works. In Project 5 the same idea routes a memory
access to RAM, to the screen, or to the keyboard.

Build `dmux4way` from three `dmux`, and `dmux8way` from one `dmux` and two
`dmux4way`.
